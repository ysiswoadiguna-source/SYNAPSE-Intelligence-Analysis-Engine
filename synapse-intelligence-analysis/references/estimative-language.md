# Estimative Language: Likelihood and Confidence

Read this when a judgment needs a likelihood statement, a confidence level, or a numeric probability.

## Why it matters

Sherman Kent found that when a 1951 National Intelligence Estimate (NIE 29-51, Yugoslavia) called an attack a "serious possibility", he meant roughly 65:35 odds, but colleagues read it as anything from 20:80 to 80:20 (Kent, "Words of Estimative Probability", *Studies in Intelligence* 8:4, 1964). Vague words hide disagreement. Later research (Mandel & Irwin, 2021, *Judgment and Decision Making*) found that verbal-only terms communicate probability worse than numbers. Heuer (ch. 12) found the same in a test of NATO officers — they agreed on the meaning of almost no verbal term — and recommended putting a numeric range in parentheses after the verbal phrase. So use one fixed scale and, when the basis supports it, give the numeric range too.

## Likelihood scale (US ICD 203, 2015 — the default)

| Range | Preferred term (EN) | Alternative (EN) | Bahasa Indonesia |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01–05% | almost no chance | remote | hampir mustahil |
| 05–20% | very unlikely | highly improbable | sangat kecil kemungkinannya |
| 20–45% | unlikely | improbable | kecil kemungkinannya |
| 45–55% | roughly even chance | roughly even odds | kurang lebih 50:50 |
| 55–80% | likely | probable | kemungkinan besar |
| 80–95% | very likely | highly probable | sangat mungkin |
| 95–99% | almost certain | nearly certain | hampir pasti |

Rules:
- Stick to one column within a product; don't mix "likely" and "probable" as if they were different.
- Pair the word with the range when the basis is defensible: "likely (55–80%)".
- "Possible" or "could" has no range; avoid it for judgments, because it says only "not zero". Use it only to describe a scenario's existence.
- Don't produce a single-point percentage ("63%") unless there is a real quantitative basis (a model, base rate, or data). A range is more honest.

Other scales exist — the UK PHIA Probability Yardstick (2025: remote chance ≤5%, highly unlikely 10–20%, unlikely 25–35%, realistic possibility 40–<50%, likely 55–75%, highly likely 80–90%, almost certain 95–<100%, with deliberate gaps) and NATO AJP-2.1 (five bands). If the user works under one of those, use theirs consistently instead.

## Confidence (separate from likelihood)

Likelihood answers "how probable?". Confidence answers "how solid is the basis for that estimate?". ICD 203 says confidence rests on the logic and the evidentiary base, including the quantity and quality of sources, and that a product must not combine a confidence level and a likelihood term in the same sentence. Write them in separate sentences:

> The customer is likely (55–80%) benchmarking prices rather than ready to buy. We have moderate confidence in this: it rests on two messages and no direct statement of budget status.

| Level | Typical basis |
|---|---|
| High | Multiple independent, reliable sources or direct evidence; few fragile assumptions; little room for deception |
| Moderate | Credible but limited or partly corroborated evidence; some important assumptions; plausible alternatives remain |
| Low | Thin, single-source, indirect, or questionable evidence; judgment rests heavily on assumptions |

(ICD 203 itself does not define the three levels; the table reflects common practice, close to the UK's criteria of information base, analytical rigour, and complexity/volatility.)

Always say what drives the uncertainty — missing information, conflicting sources, possible deception, or an inherently volatile situation.

## ICD 203 tradecraft standards (checklist)

ICD 203's analytic standards: objective; independent of political consideration; timely; based on all available sources; exhibits the tradecraft standards. The nine tradecraft standards make a useful final checklist:

1. Describe the quality and credibility of sources.
2. Express and explain uncertainty.
3. Distinguish underlying information from assumptions and judgments.
4. Incorporate analysis of alternatives.
5. Show relevance to the customer and address implications.
6. Use clear and logical argumentation.
7. Explain change to, or consistency of, judgments over time.
8. Make accurate judgments and assessments.
9. Use effective visual information where appropriate.
