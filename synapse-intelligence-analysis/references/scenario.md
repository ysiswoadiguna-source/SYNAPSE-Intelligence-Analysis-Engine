# SYNAPSE SCENARIO — Scenario Engineering

Read for future-oriented work: what could happen, how, who responds, and what to watch. Scenarios create structured preparedness under uncertainty; they are not predictions.

## 1. Doctrine

Keep apart: scenario vs prediction · desired outcome vs likely outcome · possibility vs probability · coherent narrative vs causal proof · intervention vs guaranteed result · indicator vs proof · assumption vs fact.

Heuer's warning (ch. 12): adding detail makes a scenario more convincing but *less* probable, because every added link must also occur. Vivid, detailed scenarios need the most scepticism.

## 2. Current state and objective first

Don't build futures on an unclear present. Establish (or take from ANALYST): what is happening, what is stable, changing, uncertain, contested; which actors and drivers matter; which assumptions hold the current model up.

Make the objective explicit — prepare for uncertainty, test a decision, find early warnings, anticipate an actor's response, stress-test a plan. If the objective materially changes the analysis and is unclear, ask.

## 3. Actors and perspectives

For each actor who can shape or is shaped by the outcome: objective, incentives, constraints, capabilities, authority, information (and misperceptions), risk tolerance, options, unacceptable outcomes, likely and alternative responses, unknowns.

Model each actor's own logic and information environment — not what the user would do in their place. Keep intent/motive as competing hypotheses and show how each changes the pathway.

## 4. Drivers, critical uncertainties, linchpins

**Drivers** — forces that can move the system: economic/market, policy and regulation, technology, resources, leadership, competition, organisational dynamics, public reaction, information changes, external shocks. Classify each: known/uncertain · stable/dynamic · internal/external · controllable/not · short/long-term.

**Critical uncertainties** — not every unknown matters equally. Prioritise those that are both high-impact and genuinely uncertain, can produce different pathways, and can be monitored.

**Linchpin assumptions** — those carrying a large part of a scenario. For each: why it matters → what depends on it → what would invalidate it → what happens if it fails.

## 5. Generating the scenario set

Choose the method that fits:
- **Quick archetypes** — S1 continuation (current trajectory), S2 alternative (a key assumption changes), S3 disruption (unexpected but plausible event), S4 transformation (several variables shift). Organising categories, not a probability ranking.
- **2×2 Alternative Futures** — cross the two most important *and* most uncertain drivers (`techniques.md`).
- **Cone of Plausibility** — baseline + alternatives from changed assumptions + wildcard.
- **What If? / HILP backcasting** — start from an outcome and reason backward.

Usually 3–4 scenarios. More dilutes attention; fewer hides alternatives.

Scenarios describe how the *environment* could develop (what others do, what changes around the user). The user's own possible choices ("we localise", "we cut price") are decision options to test against each scenario, not scenarios themselves — mixing them hides which future you're preparing for.

Every indicator must come from this case's actors and drivers; don't carry over generic examples.

## 6. Building each scenario

For each significant scenario specify:
- **Starting conditions** — what must already be true
- **Key drivers and critical assumptions**
- **Sequence** — T0 current state → T1 trigger → T2 actor response → T3 counter-response → T4 direct consequence → T5 adaptation → T6 second-order consequence → new state (use only stages that add understanding)
- **First- and second-order effects; feedback**
- **Failure points** — where it breaks
- **Indicators and counter-indicators**
- **Decision implications** — what the user should prepare or decide under this scenario
- **Unknowns**

## 7. Triggers, indicators, and signposts

A **trigger** moves the system from one state toward another (actor decision, policy/regulatory event, market move, leadership change, competitor action, information release, failure, shock). For each: immediate effect → affected actors → likely response → pathway.

An **indicator** is an observable sign a scenario is emerging. Rate relevance, reliability, timing, and above all **diagnosticity** — does it distinguish this scenario from the others?

> Example: a customer asks for a revised quotation. Consistent with an active purchase, price benchmarking, *and* internal approval preparation — so on its own it has low diagnosticity. A request to align delivery dates with their project schedule discriminates far better.

Strategic assumption vs tactical indicator: when a concrete current indicator contradicts a long-held assumption, trigger a scenario review rather than dismissing the indicator.

## 8. Actor responses and countermoves

At each major transition ask what every relevant actor would likely do next: likely response, alternative response, constraint, countermove. Don't assume cooperation, perfect information, perfect rationality, or passivity. Go action → response → countermove → counter-countermove only as far as it stays useful; label speculative branches.

## 9. Effects, feedback loops, branching

- **First-order:** direct consequences ("price cut → lower margin").
- **Second-order:** ask "and then what?" twice — adaptation, retaliation, substitution, new incentives, reputation, resource shifts.
- **Feedback:** reinforcing loops amplify (A→B→more A); balancing loops stabilise (A→B→less A). Note whether feedback accelerates, delays, or reverses the trajectory.
- **Branching:** create a branch only at a divergence condition that materially changes the path. Also note **convergence** — different paths that lead to the same decision requirement show which differences don't matter.
- **Dependencies:** identify linchpin variables, events, or actors; if one changes, recalculate.

## 10. Stress-testing scenarios

Try to break each scenario: Which assumption is most fragile? Which actor could behave differently? What external variable could invalidate it? What evidence would contradict it? What dependency could fail? What second-order effect was omitted? What if the opposite assumption is true? Then the coherence check: does it merely make sense, or is the mechanism supported? Run a premortem on the scenario the user is betting on.

When a scenario raises new contradictions or depends on an untested assumption, return to ANALYST to re-examine the evidence, then update.

## 11. Likelihood discipline for scenarios

Plausibility ≠ probability. Only assign likelihoods when there is a defensible basis; then use the calibrated scale (`estimative-language.md`), start from base rates, and — if the scenarios are mutually exclusive and exhaustive — make the numbers sum to 100% (add "other" if the set may be incomplete). Don't rank scenarios by drama or by what the user hopes for. A scenario's status (emerging / active / weakening / invalidated) describes its relationship to current evidence, not a guarantee.

## 12. Interventions and contingencies

When the user asks what they can do, classify variables: can control · can influence · can condition · can only monitor · cannot affect. Model intervention → actor responses → direct effect → second-order effect → unintended consequences → new state. Interventions never produce guaranteed results.

Contingency chain per meaningful scenario: early indicator → trigger → decision point → response option → fallback.

## 13. Monitoring and model revision

After building scenarios, set a monitoring path: linchpin assumptions, diagnostic indicators, triggers, actor behaviour. When new evidence arrives, ask whether it strengthens or weakens a scenario, creates a branch, breaks an assumption, or requires a new scenario. If the base has shifted, rebuild from the current state rather than patching the old model. Repeated small surprises suggest model failure.

## 14. Domain notes

- **Business:** customers, competitors, suppliers, management, employees, partners, regulators, distributors, investors — and their interactions. Useful for negotiation, tenders, pricing, market entry.
- **Intelligence/security:** intent, capability, opportunity, information environment, collection gaps, deception branches (normal vs deception vs misinterpretation vs accident). Absence of reporting is not absence of the event.
- **Political:** model institutions, coalitions, policy and legislative pathways, and electoral scenarios descriptively and neutrally, distinguishing documented fact, attributed claim, inference, and scenario. No persuasion, endorsements, voter targeting, ranking of parties/candidates, or independent election predictions.
- **Historical "what could have happened":** freeze the information set at time T; separate what was known then from what was learned later.

## 15. Output template and quality gate

Trim to fit:

1. Objective
2. Current state and current assessment (from ANALYST)
3. Actors (table) and key drivers
4. Critical uncertainties and linchpin assumptions
5. Scenario set — for each: summary, sequence, trigger, actor responses, effects, failure points, indicators, counter-indicators, decision implications
6. Comparison table (dimension × scenario: main driver, key assumption, trigger, key actor response, second-order effect, failure point, indicator)
7. Contingencies / intervention options
8. What would change the model; monitoring plan

Quality gate: current state clear? Actors and constraints modelled from their own perspective? Multiple genuinely different scenarios? Linchpins visible? Triggers explicit, indicators observable and diagnostic? Second-order effects and feedback considered? Each scenario has a condition that would invalidate it? Clearly not presented as prediction? Likelihoods (if any) calibrated and justified?
