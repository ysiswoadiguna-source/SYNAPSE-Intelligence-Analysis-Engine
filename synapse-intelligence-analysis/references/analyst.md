## 1. Observation before interpretation

Extract first, interpret second. Mixing them is how expectations leak into "facts".

- **Text:** exact claims, commitments, dates, numbers, qualifiers ("maybe", "we'll try"), what is conspicuously missing, sequence and timing of messages, contradictions with earlier statements.
- **Images:** only what is visible — text, objects, layout, symbols, anomalies. Not what it "suggests".
- **Audio/video:** exact words, speaker (only if established), timing, pauses, emphasis. A tone impression is an inference, not an observation.
- **Documents/data:** source, date, author, purpose; whether figures reconcile.

Then ask: what am I seeing because I expected to see it, and what am I missing because it doesn't fit?

## 2. Context and the information set

Interpretation depends on time, sequence, relationships, incentives, constraints, institutional setting, and what each party knew at the time. Reconstruct context from the material; label anything you supply from general knowledge as `[ASSUMED]` or `[INFERRED]`.

Memory protection: long conversations lose early details. Before judging, re-scan the material for facts mentioned once and forgotten.

## 3. Mental models and perception

Everyone simplifies reality through mental models; they are necessary and dangerous. Heuer's points to apply:
- Perception is shaped by expectation: a new reading needs *more* evidence to be recognised than an expected one.
- Mindsets form fast and change slowly; gradual drift in evidence gets assimilated into the old view.
- Initial exposure to blurred or ambiguous information hampers accurate perception later, even when better information arrives.

Self-audit for important judgments: What did I expect to see? Which interpretation appeared first, and why did it seem plausible? What evidence am I emphasising or neglecting? What would change my mind?

Mental-rut breakers: restate the question from another actor's point of view; argue the opposite; ask what an outsider with different expertise would say; consider that an apparent cause might be a consequence.

## 4. Generating hypotheses

Heuer's strategies for generating and evaluating hypotheses, each with a characteristic weakness:
- **Situational logic** — reason from the specifics of this case (risk: mirror-imaging).
- **Applying theory** — generalisations from many cases (risk: ignores case specifics).
- **Comparison / historical analogy** — "this is like X" (risk: superficial similarity).
- **Data immersion** — let patterns emerge (risk: no data is theory-free).

Use more than one when the problem matters. Generate at least three genuinely different hypotheses when ambiguity is material — including one that is unattractive or embarrassing to the user's current view, and, where relevant, "it's accidental / a misunderstanding" and "deception". Each must be testable. Don't pad with straw men.

Satisficing check — warning signs: the first explanation dominated immediately; no alternatives were generated; evidence was gathered mainly to support it; contradictory items were dismissed as noise. If present, switch to ACH.

## 5. Evidence evaluation and diagnosticity

Rate each important item on: source and access, reliability, directness (first-hand vs hearsay), specificity, timing, independence from other items (two reports from one origin are one source), possible bias or manipulation.

Then ask the central question: **does this item distinguish between hypotheses?** An item consistent with all hypotheses has low diagnostic value even if it feels important. Vivid, recent, detailed, or emotional items are not more probative for being so.

Unproven vs disproven: before treating absence of evidence as meaningful, ask "If X were true, would we realistically expect to see evidence of it here?" Consider collection gaps, concealment, timing, and access.

Persistence check: if an item that shaped your early view is later discredited, re-derive the judgment without it — impressions outlive their evidence.

## 6. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH)

Use for ambiguous, high-consequence, contested, or deception-prone questions. Heuer's eight steps (Psychology of Intelligence Analysis, ch. 8):

1. **Identify the possible hypotheses** — brainstorm widely before judging.
2. **List significant evidence and arguments** for and against each — including assumptions and the *absence* of expected evidence.
3. **Build a matrix** — hypotheses across the top, evidence down the side; assess each item's consistency with each hypothesis and its diagnosticity.
4. **Refine the matrix** — reword or combine hypotheses; drop evidence with no diagnostic value (keep a note of it).
5. **Draw tentative conclusions** — try to *disprove* hypotheses rather than prove them; work down the hypothesis columns, focusing on inconsistent items.
6. **Analyse sensitivity** — which few items or assumptions drive the result? What if they are wrong, misleading, or deceptive?
7. **Report conclusions** — discuss the relative likelihood of *all* hypotheses, not just the leader.
8. **Identify milestones** — observations that would indicate events are taking a different course.

Matrix convention:

| Evidence | Rel. | H1 | H2 | H3 | Diagnosticity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 … | High | C | C | C | Low (fits all) |
| E2 … | Med | C | I | N | High |
| E3 … | High | I | C | C | Med |

`C` consistent · `I` inconsistent · `N` neutral/N.A. · `CC`/`II` strongly. Count and weigh the `I`s: the hypothesis with the fewest serious inconsistencies survives. The matrix makes reasoning inspectable; it does not compute the answer. Note that empirical studies show ACH's benefits are mixed and depend on doing the disconfirmation and sensitivity steps honestly, not on filling the grid — see `techniques.md`.

## 7. Intent, motive, and actor modelling

Intent and motive are inferred, never observed. For each significant actor:

| Dimension | Notes |
|---|---|
| Stated objective | what they actually said (quote) |
| Observable behaviour | actions, timing, sequence |
| Incentives & pressures | money, career, status, deadlines, organisation |
| Constraints & authority | what they can and cannot decide |
| Information | what they know/don't know; what they may misperceive |
| Options | what alternatives they have |
| Intent hypotheses | H1/H2/H3 with supporting & contradicting evidence |
| Unknowns | what would discriminate |

Keep three layers apart: what the actor said → what they appear to want → why they might want it.

Motive dimensions to consider: incentive, self-interest, resource pressure, fear/risk reduction, status, values, organisational pressure, career, relationship, conflict, opportunity, external pressure, misunderstanding, error. Don't collapse to one motive while several remain plausible; ask what evidence distinguishes them.

**Mirror-imaging defence:** don't ask only "what would I do?" Ask "what makes sense given *their* information, values, constraints, and risk tolerance?" Behaviour that looks irrational from outside is often rational inside the actor's situation.

**Attribution check:** before attributing behaviour to character or intent, test situation, constraints, process, incentives, and timing. Observers over-attribute others' behaviour to disposition and their own to circumstance.

## 8. Assumptions and linchpins

List explicit and hidden assumptions — about actors, incentives, "normal" behaviour, causality, processes, timing. Run a Key Assumptions Check (`techniques.md`) on the important ones.

Linchpins are the few assumptions or evidence items that actually drive the conclusion. For each: why it matters, what rests on it, how reliable it is, what the alternative reading is, consequences if wrong, and what evidence could test it. Don't let peripheral detail hide the variables that decide the judgment.

Strategic assumption vs tactical indicator: when a concrete, current indicator contradicts a long-held assumption, don't dismiss the indicator — ask whether the assumption has expired.

## 9. Deception check

Treat deception as a hypothesis when it is plausible — not a default, not something ruled out because direct evidence is absent. Consider motive, opportunity, means, cost/benefit to the deceiver, source access, whether key evidence comes through a channel the other side controls, and whether the absence of evidence is itself explainable. Contradictory evidence alone is not a sign of deception.

## 10. Causality and attribution

Sequence is not cause. Test mechanism, alternative causes, external factors, organisational process, incentives, human error, coincidence, and multiple interacting causes. Heuer warns of a bias toward causal explanations: people prefer an orderly story to randomness, and assume centralised direction ("they planned this") where accident, bureaucratic process, or many uncoordinated actors are as likely. Also beware assuming big effects must have big causes. Ask: "Is there a mechanism, or just a coherent story?"

## 11. Contradictions, surprises, and model failure

Contradiction: when E1 says X and E2 says not-X, compare reliability, directness, timing, independence, access, bias; check whether both can be true under different conditions or times. "Unresolved" is a valid result.

Surprise (expected ≠ observed): verify the observation and source, check timing, review assumptions, generate alternatives, compare models, revise if warranted. A series of small surprises often signals a larger model failure — the classic pattern before strategic surprise.

Incremental analysis trap: when new evidence trickles in, it gets fitted into the existing view. After significant new evidence, reassess from the ground up rather than appending.

## 12. Information value: what to get next

Prioritise information that would change the judgment. Ask: what decision is being improved; which uncertainty matters; would this item discriminate between hypotheses; what does it cost to get? Recommend the one to three highest-value questions or checks, phrased so the user can act on them (e.g. the exact question to ask a customer).

## 13. Deep-tier report structure

Use and trim as needed:

1. Analytical question
2. Bottom line (judgment, likelihood, confidence, key uncertainty)
3. Key observations and sourced facts (tagged)
4. Context and actors (actor table)
5. Competing hypotheses
6. ACH matrix and diagnostic evidence
7. Intent/motive assessment (if relevant)
8. Key assumptions and linchpins
9. Contradictions and gaps
10. Bias audit (mechanism → effect → correction)
11. Current judgment and sensitivity
12. What would change the assessment (per hypothesis)
13. Indicators / next information to collect

Audit trail: another analyst should be able to see why the judgment was reached.

## 14. Handoff to SCENARIO

When the question turns to the future, pass the state, not just the verdict: current state, actors and objectives, intent/motive hypotheses with relative standing, critical evidence, contradictions, linchpin assumptions, key drivers and constraints, uncertainties, and indicators.
